Findings, ranked by revenue impact

01

One in eight orders clicks “Place My Order” more than once

Revenue 67 order attempts Already ticketed

Of 67 US order attempts, 8 clicked the order button two or more times. The gaps between clicks are the important part: they run from 17 to 97 seconds, not fractions of a second. This is not a fat-finger double-tap. It is someone pressing the button, seeing nothing happen, waiting, and pressing it again.

The fix is already specified in work item 345, which traced it to the button staying live while validation makes a network round trip, and asks for a pending state on the first click, an in-flight guard, and invoice reuse server side. Nothing here changes that.

What the recordings add is scope. Four of these eight order codes do not appear in the ticket’s invoice table, so the count of shoppers hitting this is higher than the Stripe side alone shows. Clicks and invoices also do not match one to one: O7PN27 took four clicks and produced no invoice row, which looks like validation failing rather than the guard holding, while O4GR18 produced three invoices from two observed clicks. Neither source is complete on its own.

02

Adding to cart almost never becomes an order

Revenue 130 cart sessions

130 US sessions fired an add-to-cart event. Four reached /thank-you/. Of the rest, 36 ended sitting on the checkout page itself and 25 walked back out to /std-test-pricing/.

Those two exits say different things. Ending on checkout is a form or payment problem. Returning to the pricing page after seeing the total looks like price reaction, and it is the single most common backwards move in the data. Worth watching alongside the in-home collection fee, which nearly doubles some orders.

03

“GET RESULTS FAST” is the most dead-clicked thing on the site

Quick fix 14 dead clicks 3 pages

It is dead-clicked on the homepage, on /tests/, and on /popular-lab-tests/. Checked against the live page at origin, it is a <p class="how-it-works-step-title"> inside the How It Works explainer. It is not a link and never was.

People read the three steps as three things they can do and click the last one. Either make the step cards link somewhere sensible, or stop styling them like affordances. Same pattern shows up on the neighbouring step text and on “My Cart Your cart is empty” on the homepage, clicked dead three times.

04

Checkout scores 2 out of 100 on performance

Technical 323 page loads

Across 323 US checkout page loads the mean Clarity performance score is 2.2. Largest Contentful Paint has a median of 1.8s, a 90th percentile of 3.4s and a worst case of 5.6s.

Every checkout entry also loads twice. The lowercase order URL appears first for roughly a second, then the uppercase one. A case redirect on the highest-value page on the site, paid for on every single order.

05

Quick-backs cluster almost entirely on the homepage

Acquisition 250 sessions

Of 250 sessions containing a quick-back, 143 entered on the homepage. Nothing else comes close; the runner-up is /tests/ with 6.

A quick-back is a click followed by an immediate return, so this is people landing on the homepage, choosing something, and reversing out of it. It reads as a navigation or expectation mismatch one click deep, not a landing page that fails on arrival.

06

Rage clicks are effectively absent, which changes what to watch

Method 1 session

One US rage-click session in the whole window. That is genuinely good, but it also means rage clicks are the wrong alarm to wire up here.

Dead clicks are the signal on this site, at 273 in a comparable slice. Any recurring monitor should track dead clicks and repeat-click gaps, and ignore rage.

Known issues with pulling data this way

Five things constrain the pull itself. They shape how far the numbers above can be pushed, and all five are worth fixing before this becomes a recurring report.

  • The 250-session cap is the biggest limit. Every query returns at most 250 sessions, newest first, with no pagination. On a busy filter that is a few hours of traffic, not the week that was requested. Widening a date range does nothing; only a narrower filter reaches further back.
  • Country can only be filtered on the way in. The filter is inclusion-only, with no way to exclude, and the response carries no per-session country to sort on afterwards. So this is United States rather than everything-minus-offshore. Getting it wrong is not harmless: an unfiltered first pass produced three false double-submits that vanished once India and the Philippines were dropped.
  • Masked fields fake repeat clicks. Payment inputs come back as bullet characters, so ordinary typing in the card field reads as a burst of clicks on one element. Anything measuring repeat clicks has to discard them or it will find friction that is not there. Discarded here.
  • Nothing separates internal traffic from customers. Known QA orders from a Bellevue address sit in the data with no flag distinguishing them, and they place real-looking orders. Blocking those IPs in Clarity settings is the only clean fix, and it would improve every future pull.
  • Events arrive unlabelled as to intent. A dead click is only a record that a click hit nothing, not a judgement that something is broken; some are people clicking body copy to select it. Separating the two is manual, which is why the findings above are limited to patterns that repeat across sessions and pages.

What I would do next

  • Add the four missing order codes to work item 345, since the fix is already scoped there but the affected-order count is understated.
  • Kill the checkout case redirect so the page loads once.
  • Decide whether the How It Works steps are links; right now they are neither clearly.
  • Block internal QA IPs in Clarity so the sample stops being polluted.
  • Run the checkout and dead-click queries weekly and track the repeat-click rate as the number that matters.
